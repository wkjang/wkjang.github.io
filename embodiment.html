Embodied Representation with Computer Vision

Computer vision measures how bodies are shown and how they move: posture, interpersonal distance, limb angles, and body spread. I apply it to everyday play video and to the media children watch, then read the patterns through social semiotics.

Research question
How are bodies and identities represented in children's media and play, and what do children learn from it?
Data
Children's media and everyday play
Contexts
Media, home, and classroom
Approach
Computer vision with social semiotics

In depth

Bodies, media, and social meaning

Children's media and picture books teach through bodies as well as words: who is shown moving, leading, climbing, or taking risks, and who is shown still and watching.

Pose estimation and posture analysis make these patterns measurable across home play video and children's media, and social semiotics explains how repeated action patterns make certain roles feel natural.

This connects critical literacy with everyday practice. Teachers and families can use these patterns to choose texts and media thoughtfully and to invite children to notice and question how characters are represented.

Critical, human-centered lens

Who is seen, who decides

Social semiotics

Who moves, leads, and takes risks on screen teaches children what bodies like theirs can do.

Critical big data & sociotechnical

Media samples and pose models are audited for whose bodies are represented and how accurately they are detected.

Human-centered AI

Findings go back to teachers and families as prompts for choosing media and inviting children to question representation.

How it works

From signal to evidence

step 1

Detect

Find every person or character and estimate keypoints, even on stylized animation.

step 2

Classify posture

Label standing, sitting, lying, and active movement such as running, climbing, or using tools.

step 3

Quantify motion

Measure limb angles, body spread, and range of motion as continuous features.

step 4

Interpret

Aggregate by character gender and role, and read repeated action patterns through social semiotics.

Findings

What the analyses show

Male characters are more often shown running, jumping, climbing, and operating tools, with larger limb angles and wider body spread that signal agency and action.

Female characters are more often shown standing or sitting, with smaller movements, closed postures, and a limited range of motion.

Read through social semiotics, these repeated action patterns make certain roles feel natural and shape expectations about who gets to move, lead, and take risks.
