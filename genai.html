Auditing Generative AI for Children (PLACE-AI)

PLACE-AI asks what generative AI produces when educators ask it for early-grade, place-based curricula. Model outputs are broken into units, embedded, matched to disciplinary constructs, and classified only when the evidence is clear. Uncertain units are set aside instead of forced into a label.

Research question
What does generative AI teach children about place, discipline, and culture?

In depth

Critical, place-based curriculum and generative AI

Community-engaged curriculum work connects literacy, science, social studies, the arts, and history through locally meaningful questions about weather, energy, land, and community life, in partnership with educators, families, librarians, museum educators, and colleagues in meteorology and history. A child's interest in a storm or a wind turbine can lead to informational reading, explanatory talk, diagramming, new vocabulary, and collaborative writing.

Critical curriculum inquiry asks whose account of a place is represented, which voices and histories are missing, and how local and Indigenous knowledge can be engaged through respectful partnership rather than simply added to a lesson.

PLACE-AI extends this approach to generative AI. Computational analysis and qualitative review of AI-generated lessons examine how disciplinary knowledge is represented and whose cultural, historical, and community perspectives are absent or distorted. Preliminary analyses suggest disciplinary imbalances and show that intentional place-based prompting can broaden what models include; these are emerging results, not evidence that AI can produce culturally sustaining instruction on its own.

Next steps apply the approach to children's literature, informational books, digital stories, and AI-generated reading lessons. Review by teachers, families, librarians, and community experts remains essential to tell a fluent-looking output from instruction that is accurate, appropriate, and worthy of children's trust.

Critical, human-centered lens

Who is seen, who decides

Social semiotics

AI-generated lessons are designed texts whose words, images, and examples make some meanings easier to see than others.

Critical big data & sociotechnical

Model outputs reflect what was in their training data. Audits measure disciplinary and cultural imbalance and how prompting shifts it.

Human-centered AI

Educators, families, and community experts review outputs alongside the audit and decide what is accurate and worthy of children's trust.

Across fields

One question, several lenses

Early literacies

AI now writes children's texts

Generative AI produces reading passages, lesson plans, and stories for young children. What these texts include, and leave out, becomes part of children's literacy environment, so it deserves the same scrutiny as any picture book or curriculum.

Digital literacies

Reading and questioning machine-made text

Children and teachers increasingly meet AI in apps, search, and classroom tools. Digital and critical literacies mean learning to ask where a text came from, whose knowledge it reflects, and how prompts and interfaces shape what appears, for adults and, in age-appropriate ways, for children.

Human development

What children come to see as knowledge

Curricula signal which places, people, and ways of knowing count. Repeated over time, these signals shape the identities and futures children can imagine, which makes AI a cultural institution in their development, not a neutral tool.

Multimodal AI

Auditing models with models

Transformer embeddings, prototype retrieval, and selective classification measure disciplinary balance and cultural representation across thousands of AI-generated units, with abstention when evidence is weak.

How it works

From signal to evidence

step 1

Generate

Prompt a model under four conditions, from a topic-only default to fully integrated place-based instructions.

step 2

Encode

Split outputs into sentence or activity units and embed each with a transformer.

step 3

Retrieve

Compare each unit against STEM and social studies prototypes.

step 4

Decide

Label a unit only when it is relevant and unambiguous; otherwise abstain.

step 5

Measure

Report disciplinary balance, how units follow one another, and which ideas cluster together.
